## Introduction

OpenSpliceAI implements the SpliceAI architecture in PyTorch and supports retraining with different
annotations and species [2]. Similar splice-site prediction performance does not establish that two
trained models assign the same effect to a sequence variant. Here, we compare OpenSpliceAI and
SpliceAI across the published collection of masked genic single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), asking
where their predicted effects agree and which types of effects differ most.

We distinguish four events: **acceptor gain (AG), acceptor loss (AL), donor gain (DG), and donor loss
(DL)**. Gains summarize increases in predicted splice-site scores after substitution; losses summarize
decreases. Each event score records the largest change within the search window and has an associated
selected position. A variant can receive multiple nonzero event scores. These events are therefore
not mutually exclusive variant classes. MAX, the largest of the four scores, summarizes predicted
effect strength without distinguishing gain from loss or acceptor from donor.

Our comparisons address score magnitude, overlap of calls at a common cutoff, boundary proximity,
and selected positions. We also compare two OpenSpliceAI training seeds on identical observations.
SpliceAI is the comparator throughout. The collection contains no experimental splicing outcomes,
so concordance identifies similarities and differences between predictions without determining which
model is correct when they disagree.

## Study design and inference progress

The campaign divides the published SpliceAI masked genic SNV collection into {{study.source_chunks|,d}}
chunks. Here, genome-wide describes the collection's genomic extent; the collection does not contain
every possible substitution at every GRCh38 base. OpenSpliceAI scoring uses two MANE-trained
checkpoints, rs10 and rs13, with a 10,000-nt context. These are training seeds, not cross-validation
folds. Each comparison uses one OpenSpliceAI checkpoint against the published five-model SpliceAI
ensemble.

Campaign records specify GRCh38.p14, the GENCODE v24 canonical gene table supplied with SpliceAI,
a ±{{study.distance|d}}-bp event-search window, and masked output. The scoring annotation controls
gene assignment and masking; MANE supplied the OpenSpliceAI training annotation. Matching the former
does not match the training data. The legacy outputs passed structural audits but lack embedded
checkpoint receipts. We therefore retain their recorded model labels and provisional provenance,
without treating file validity as independent proof of checkpoint identity.

We pair annotations by chromosome, position, reference allele, alternate allele, and gene. Identical
repeats are collapsed; conflicting duplicates are excluded. Missing scores remain missing. This gives
{{primary.coverage.paired_annotations|,d}} paired variant–gene annotations from {{primary.chunks|,d}}
rs10 chunks for the primary comparison. A physical VCF row, a distinct variant allele, and a
variant–gene annotation are different counting units. All main event statistics use paired
annotations unless stated otherwise.

The seed analysis uses {{arms.B_seeds_rs10_rs13.coverage.paired_annotations|,d}} observations shared by
SpliceAI, rs10 and rs13 after duplicate resolution and joining groups split across chunk boundaries.
The rs10–rs13 and both SpliceAI–OpenSpliceAI contrasts use this identical intersection. It covers
{{study.rs13_genomic_extent}} and is not a random sample of the genome.

## Loss scores agree more closely than gain scores

Both acceptors and donors show greater score agreement for losses than gains (Figure 2). Pearson
correlations are {{primary.agreement.AL.pearson_r|.3f}} for AL and
{{primary.agreement.DL.pearson_r|.3f}} for DL, compared with {{primary.agreement.AG.pearson_r|.3f}}
for AG and {{primary.agreement.DG.pearson_r|.3f}} for DG. Donor gain has the lowest correlation,
whereas the two loss correlations are similar. This gain–loss distinction is more informative than
the single MAX correlation of {{primary.agreement.MAX.pearson_r|.3f}}.

<!-- figure:main02_score_agreement -->

The conditional distributions show how this difference depends on score magnitude. For losses,
the median OpenSpliceAI score follows the equal-score line more closely. For gains, it lies below
that line across much of the SpliceAI range, especially for donor gain. The intervals contain the
central 50% and 90% of scores within each comparator bin. Their width shows that the median is not
a prediction for every annotation; OpenSpliceAI does not always assign the lower gain score.

Across the full paired population, mean signed differences, defined as OpenSpliceAI minus SpliceAI,
are negative for both gains and slightly positive for both losses. Mean absolute differences are
{{primary.agreement.AG.mae|.5f}} for AG, {{primary.agreement.AL.mae|.5f}} for AL,
{{primary.agreement.DG.mae|.5f}} for DG and {{primary.agreement.DL.mae|.5f}} for DL. These averages
include the large population of low and zero scores. Small global mean differences can therefore
coexist with substantial differences among annotations assigned a high score by either model.

Output precision affects exact equality. SpliceAI reports two decimals and OpenSpliceAI five.
Rounding OpenSpliceAI to two decimals increases exact matches, while a sensitivity analysis that
subtracts a 0.005 allowance from each absolute difference retains substantial residual error
(Figure S4). This calculation does not estimate how much disagreement was caused by rounding.
We interpret the conditional distributions together with positive-call overlap, rather than using
exact equality alone to assess whether the score collections can be substituted for one another.

## Call overlap differs between the four events

We define a call as an event score at or above a specified cutoff. Jaccard overlap is the number of
annotations called by both models divided by the number called by either model. It excludes shared
negative calls. The call-count ratio divides OpenSpliceAI calls by SpliceAI calls and measures their
relative number, independently of whether those calls identify the same annotations (Figure 3).

At 0.5, loss-call overlap is higher than gain-call overlap for both acceptors and donors. OpenSpliceAI
makes {{revision.call_changes.AL|.1%}} more AL calls and {{revision.call_changes.DL|.1%}} more DL calls than SpliceAI. In contrast, it makes {{revision.call_changes.AG|.1%}} fewer AG
calls and {{revision.call_changes.DG|.1%}} fewer DG calls. Donor gain has both the lowest Jaccard overlap and the smallest
relative call count. The gain–loss distinction is thus shared by the two splice-site types, while
the magnitude of gain disagreement is greater for donors.

Call overlap and relative call count answer different questions. Equal counts can still describe disjoint sets, while unequal counts can coexist with a large shared subset. Reporting both quantities makes this distinction visible for each event.

These percentages describe the chosen cutoff. They do not establish that one model always makes
more or fewer calls. For example, the MAX call-count ratio changes from
{{primary.thresholds.MAX[0.2].call_rate_ratio_right_over_left|.3f}} at 0.2 to
{{primary.thresholds.MAX[0.8].call_rate_ratio_right_over_left|.3f}} at 0.8. The curves show approximate
histogram-based cutoffs; the emphasized 0.5 points use exact counters. The supplementary threshold
analysis separately selects cutoffs by call-volume matching and by agreement with SpliceAI, because
similar call counts do not ensure similar membership.

A smaller call count does not establish higher specificity, and a larger count does not establish
higher sensitivity. Either conclusion would require experimental outcomes. Likewise, the dominant
event—the largest score within a model—is a useful descriptive label but discards the other three
scores. Figure S5 retains ties and all-zero annotations to show that compression explicitly.

## Boundary proximity distinguishes local patterns

We grouped variants by distance to the nearest annotated internal splice boundary. The index contains
{{study.sites_count|,d}} unique coordinates, assigns acceptor and donor roles by strand, pools across
genes, and excludes transcript ends. Coordinates assigned both roles are classified as ambiguous.
Figure 4 pools counts across all three boundary types before dividing by the number of paired
annotations in each distance group.

<!-- figure:main04_boundary_context -->

At a cutoff of 0.5, loss-call volumes are similar within 1–2 bp of a boundary: OpenSpliceAI/SpliceAI
ratios are {{revision.boundary_ratios.AL[1-2]|.3f}} for AL and {{revision.boundary_ratios.DL[1-2]|.3f}} for DL. The models differ more in the 11–50-bp group, where the
ratios are {{revision.boundary_ratios.AL[11-50]|.2f}} for AL and {{revision.boundary_ratios.DL[11-50]|.2f}} for DL. Thus, the higher overall number of OpenSpliceAI loss calls
does not correspond to a uniform increase at every distance. The donor-loss increase is particularly
pronounced in the 11–50-bp group, even though global AL call counts increase more than DL counts.

For gains, OpenSpliceAI makes fewer calls at 0.5 in every displayed distance group. Beyond 500 bp,
the ratio is {{revision.boundary_ratios.AG.far|.3f}} for AG and {{revision.boundary_ratios.DG.far|.3f}} for DG. Donor gain therefore shows the larger relative reduction
in this distant group. The plotted rates use within-group denominators; the much larger number of
distant annotations affects total call counts but does not automatically increase a within-group
rate. Conditional gain-score medians also lie closer to equal scores near boundaries than in the
distant group across much of the comparator range (Figure 4E–F).

Boundary distance and masking describe different quantities. The recorded masking procedure selects
the nearest exon boundary for a particular variant–gene annotation, including transcript ends and
pooling boundary types. It retains a loss only at that selected boundary and suppresses a gain there.
Masking acts on the selected event position, whereas our distance groups use the variant position
and a pooled internal-boundary index. Their boundary sets are not identical. Sparse distant loss
calls therefore cannot be interpreted as an independent measurement of absent biological losses.

The symmetric 1–2-bp group includes both sides of a boundary and is not equivalent to the essential
splice dinucleotides. A distance greater than 500 bp also does not establish a deep-intronic location;
we did not assign transcript-specific intronic or exonic status. These groups locate differences in
predictions without identifying their biological cause. Figures S6–S8 provide the full distributions,
conditional intervals, and eligible position-comparison counts.

## Jointly called gains usually select the same position

Among annotations with both event scores at least 0.5, exact selected-position agreement is
{{revision.dp.AG.exact|pct2}} for AG and {{revision.dp.DG.exact|pct2}} for DG (Figure 5). Many gain
calls are unique to one model, but the shared subset usually selects the same position. Score
magnitude and selected location therefore provide distinct descriptions of model disagreement.

<!-- figure:main05_positions -->

For losses, exact agreement is 1,441,729 of 1,441,730 AL annotations and 1,630,983 of 1,630,987 DL
annotations. These correspond to one AL mismatch and four DL mismatches, which would be hidden by
rounding to two percentage decimals. The AL mismatch is within 2 bp; all four DL mismatches are
within 5 bp. We report the exceptions explicitly instead of describing loss-position agreement as
perfect. All four DL exceptions map to TVP23C-CDRT4 and the AL exception to XAGE3. Source-record checks confirm that OpenSpliceAI selects the current annotation boundary in both genes, whereas the published SpliceAI position differs by 3 bp and 2 bp, respectively.

The recorded mask restricts retained loss positions, so near-complete loss agreement is not an
independent test of loss localization. The two legacy score collections do not report identical loss positions in every case; the origin of these local discrepancies remains unresolved. More generally, selecting the same position does not establish splice-site use in
vivo. Position agreement is conditional on shared calls and excludes predictions unique to either
model. It must be considered alongside call overlap and score magnitude, with undefined agreement
retained for distance groups lacking jointly called events.

## Gain disagreement exceeds the observed seed difference

We compared the mean absolute difference between rs10 and rs13 with the difference between each
checkpoint and SpliceAI on the identical three-way intersection (Figure 6). This controls the
comparison domain: differences between these contrasts cannot arise simply because they contain
different variant–gene observations. It does not match the training data or ensemble size.

<!-- figure:main06_seed_comparison -->

For rs10, the SpliceAI–OpenSpliceAI difference divided by the seed difference is
{{seed_versus_model.events.AG.ratios.C_rs10_matched.mae|.2f}} for AG and
{{seed_versus_model.events.DG.ratios.C_rs10_matched.mae|.2f}} for DG, compared with
{{seed_versus_model.events.AL.ratios.C_rs10_matched.mae|.2f}} for AL and
{{seed_versus_model.events.DL.ratios.C_rs10_matched.mae|.2f}} for DL. The rs13 contrasts show the same
pattern. Gain differences between the model collections are therefore larger relative to this
observed seed contrast, particularly for donor gain. Loss differences are closer in magnitude to
the difference between the two OpenSpliceAI checkpoints.

Gene-level ratios provide a complementary description. Among genes with at least 1,000 matched
annotations and positive, defined ratios, more than 99.8% of AG ratios and 99.9% of DG ratios exceed
one for both checkpoints (Figure S9). Loss ratios are more mixed. These curves weight genes equally,
whereas the global mean differences weight annotations. A very small seed difference can also
produce a large ratio, so extreme ratios should not be interpreted as evidence of a large absolute
effect or a particular mechanism.

Two seeds provide one observed training contrast, not a population estimate of training variability.
A ratio near one does not establish interchangeability or show that seed variation explains the
model difference. Training annotations, checkpoints, implementation, and ensembling remain combined
in the comparison. The matched domain is also restricted to part of the genome, limiting
extrapolation to unscored regions. Regional and gene-level MAX summaries remain descriptive
supporting analyses (Figures S10–S11).

## Discussion

The comparison identifies a consistent gain–loss distinction for both acceptors and donors. Loss
scores and calls agree more closely between the models. Gain scores show larger differences, with
donor gain showing the lowest correlation and call overlap. Among shared gain calls, selected
positions usually agree. Boundary context further distinguishes similar loss-call volumes close to
splice boundaries from larger differences farther away. These results explain why overall MAX
correlation alone cannot describe agreement across the four predicted events.

Our eLife paper [2] evaluated splice-site prediction, mutagenesis around selected sites, and
cryptic-splicing examples. Its mutagenesis comparisons used five-model ensembles. The present study
instead compares individual OpenSpliceAI checkpoints with the published SpliceAI ensemble across
masked genic variants. The sampling, averaging, and predicted quantities differ. Correlations from
those experiments should therefore not be treated as interchangeable performance measures.

Determining which discordant predictions correspond to observed splicing changes requires independent
outcomes covering shared calls and calls unique to each model. A comparison with matched ensemble
sizes would help separate ensemble effects from the remaining model differences. The current
analysis identifies where those evaluations would be informative; it does not rank biological
accuracy or establish a causal explanation for the observed gain–loss pattern.

## Methods and data availability

We summarized paired annotations with additive counters, moments, fixed-width histograms, and
stratum tables. Configured cutoffs of 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8 use exact counters; 200-bin
histograms of width 0.005 provide approximate curves and conditional quantiles. Independent
implementations passed {{study.verification.primary.checks|,d}} primary and
{{study.verification.matched.checks|,d}} matched numerical checks on selected real-data windows.
Those windows verify implementation behavior on their inputs, rather than every genomic record.
